I prefer to update LinkedIn before I need a new job. A good LinkedIn profile lets
recruiters find me while I am busy doing my actual job. I do not have to know that a role exists,
follow the company or happen to search on the right week. My profile can appear when somebody looks
for a software engineer in Singapore with a particular mix of backend and AI infrastructure work.

LinkedIn Recruiter is built for exactly this. Recruiters can filter by job title, skills and location,
and the Keywords field searches the full profile. Its matching features also use titles, skills and
location. [LinkedIn describes those surfaces publicly](https://business.linkedin.com/hire/recruiter/recruiter-features),
although it does not publish a magic weighting formula for each field.

That changes the starting point of a hiring process. A cold application asks a general queue to notice
me. An inbound recruiter has already matched some part of my background to an open role. I still have
to decide whether the role is any good and pass the same interviews, but the first filter has already
happened.

My own numbers made this hard to ignore. Before my first serious profile pass, LinkedIn showed 49
search appearances and 103 profile views over seven days, with no post impressions. Over the same
seven-day window after the changes, profile views rose to about 270, roughly 2.6 times the baseline.
Eight recruiter messages also arrived the day after I made the changes. The view increase is measured;
one day of messages does not prove the edits caused all eight. It was enough to make me take the profile more seriously, even though I was not posting.

A later snapshot was even more lopsided. Ten of 11 leads from in-house recruiters reached a real
hiring process. None of my three self-applied or outbound applications in the same early sample had
replied. The sample was small and the numbers have changed as the search continued. I would not turn
91% versus 0% into a universal claim. It did make spending a few hours on the profile seem worthwhile.

This does not mean LinkedIn replaces applications. If I want a specific role, I still apply. A strong
referral is better than either route. LinkedIn adds another way for an opportunity to begin, and that
way keeps working when I am not actively searching.

It also improves the first conversation. When the headline, About section, experience and skills all
describe the same person, a recruiter can approach with a relevant role and explain my fit to a hiring
manager. We can spend the call on the team's mandate, compensation and mutual fit instead of working
out what kind of engineer I am.

## How I do it

### 1. Decide which searches I want to appear in

I start with the job, not the wording. I take a small set of roles I would genuinely accept and look
for the titles, skills and systems that recur across them. Then I keep only the terms I can defend from
real work.

My current direction is backend, AI infrastructure and forward-deployed engineering in Singapore.
That is narrow enough to produce a coherent profile and broad enough to catch several reasonable job
titles. If I tried to rank for backend engineer, ML researcher, product manager and engineering leader
at once, the result would tell a recruiter nothing.

The first screen should answer four questions without requiring a click:

- What role do I do?
- What am I unusually good at?
- Where am I based?
- What evidence makes that believable?

### 2. Save the baseline

Before changing anything, I record the previous seven days of search appearances, profile views, total
recruiter messages and qualified recruiter messages. The last number matters most. Ten irrelevant
agency blasts are worse than one message from a team I would join.

I can spend an hour rewriting a paragraph without knowing whether it helped. Saving a baseline gives
me something to compare with afterwards.

### 3. Fix the fields used for retrieval

I work through four fields first.

**Headline.** I lead with a standard occupation, then add the specialty and a few technologies that
define the work. A recruiter is more likely to search for "Software Engineer" than a clever slogan.

**Location.** I use the place where I can actually work. Recruiters filter on it directly.

**Open to Work.** I use recruiter-only mode and fill the available title slots with distinct roles I
would accept, rather than five spellings of the same job. LinkedIn says it tries to hide this signal
from recruiters at your current company, but [it cannot guarantee complete
privacy](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a507508). That is a real tradeoff if you are
employed.

**Skills.** I put the target identity first and associate each important skill with the job where I
used it. I remove old skills that attract work I no longer want. LinkedIn may allow a long list, but the
maximum is not a target.

Keyword stuffing is easy to spot. The profile still has to make sense to the person who opens it.

### 4. Make the rest of the profile prove the headline

The About section explains the same positioning in normal sentences. The experience section proves it
with systems built, scope owned and outcomes achieved. I use recognizable, truthful titles and explain
an obscure internal title in the description instead of expecting outsiders to decode it.

If my work changes materially, I split the roles so the current scope is searchable. I do not invent a
promotion because a better title would rank well. Searchability is useful only while the profile stays
credible.

I also draw a firm line around what becomes public. Architecture, technologies and broad scope are
usually safe. Confidential business metrics are not. A public profile has to survive being read by a
recruiter, a colleague and my current employer.

This is where *I Hate Job Interviews* by Sam Owens becomes useful. One of its central ideas is the
"power example": connect something the employer needs to a concrete example of past work. The
profile carries the compressed claim. I keep the full story ready for the interview, including the
tradeoff, what I personally did and what changed.

### 5. Add examples of the work

I would rather show work I have done than post just to stay visible. Useful proof includes a specific recommendation
from somebody who saw the work, a strong public project, a personal site and a resume that agrees with
the profile.

I do not maintain a posting calendar for recruiter SEO. LinkedIn does not publish evidence that a
weekly stream of generic career posts improves Recruiter search rank. My first inbound burst arrived
with zero post impressions anyway. If I have something worth writing, I write it here.

### 6. Check privacy and consistency

Before editing, I turn off **Share profile updates with your network**. A new role or headline can be a
very loud signal while employed.

After editing, I read the profile on desktop and mobile. Dates, titles and public claims should agree
with my resume, GitHub and personal site. I remove anything confidential, stale or likely to create an
interview question I cannot answer well.

### 7. Measure for four weeks, then leave it alone

I track the same four numbers once a week and classify every message by quality. I avoid making another
large profile change or starting a new posting routine during the test, because then I would have no
idea what produced the result.

Four weeks is enough to identify obvious wrong-role attractors and see whether qualified inbound moved.
It is not enough to calculate a scientifically clean interview conversion rate. Hiring is too lumpy
for that.

Then I stop polishing and work the opportunities. I reply, qualify the role, research the company,
prepare my examples and keep a next action against every live process. Dana Manciagli's *Cut the Crap,
Get a Job!* is good on this point: a job search is a pipeline, not a sequence of hopes. Her companion
guidance recommends tracking multiple active opportunities, networking around each role and following
up until each one reaches a clear outcome. [Her explanation of the "odds
game" is available here](https://www.danamanciagli.com/blog-post-15.html).

## Why I review it once a year

I think a deliberate LinkedIn review once a year is enough, even if I am happy in my job. I do it after
reviewing my resume and accomplishments, when the year's strongest work and my next direction are
already clear.

Annual maintenance works for a few reasons. My experience changes. The vocabulary used in job
descriptions moves. A skill that helped me get my current role may attract the wrong role three years
later. Updating while employed is also much calmer than rebuilding everything after deciding I need to
leave.

There is value even if I reject every message. Inbound shows which parts of my background the market is
buying, which companies are hiring in Singapore and what compensation ranges are appearing. A useful
conversation can become a relationship long before it becomes a job search.

Between annual reviews, I update only when reality changes: a promotion, materially different scope, a
major public project, a move to another country or a real shift in the work I would accept. I do not
rewrite the profile every month because search appearances moved around.

My annual pass is short:

1. Update the raw accomplishments and resume facts.
2. Read 10 relevant job descriptions and note repeated language.
3. Refresh the headline, About, experience and skills as one story.
4. Check privacy, links and consistency with the rest of my public presence.
5. Measure qualified inbound for four weeks.
6. Leave it alone until something real changes.

## Preparing for the conversations

The profile helps people find me. After that, I still need to assess the role, prepare examples and
practise for the interview. Two books have been useful for that:

- [*I Hate Job Interviews* by Sam Owens](https://www.harpercollinsleadership.com/9781400245901/i-hate-job-interviews/)
  is organised around confidence, inside information, power examples and practising with humans. I like
  that it treats interviewing as a performance skill that can be trained.
- [*Cut the Crap, Get a Job!* by Dana Manciagli](https://www.barnesandnoble.com/w/cut-the-crap-get-a-job-a-new-job-search-process-for-a-new-era-dana-manciagli/1115054817)
  treats the whole search as an operating system: goals, preparation, applications, interviews, tracking
  and follow-up. It is direct and practical.

Once the profile is current, I would spend the next hour preparing for a real conversation rather
than rewriting the headline again.
